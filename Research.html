Contested Development: Land Reform Sequences and the Remaking of Inequality in Latin America

In the early 1960s, governments across Latin America adopted a remarkably similar liberal land reform blueprint. Yet within a decade, some reforms had been reinforced, others radicalized, and others reversed. Contested Development explains how the interaction between mobilized rural constituencies, agrarian elites, and competing development paradigms produced these divergent reform sequences.

Anchored in a comparison of Colombia and Peru, the book shows that these politically contingent sequences mattered far beyond the distribution of land. They reshaped the power of landed and economic elites, the redistributive and coercive capacities of the state, and the extent to which redistribution became embedded in public institutions. Drawing on comparative historical analysis, counterfactual reasoning, and quantitative analysis of subnational patterns, the book traces how land reform sequences created different long-run equilibria of inequality, redistribution, and violence.
